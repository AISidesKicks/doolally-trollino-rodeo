# Inside LFM: policy and content-risk classification

Model: `LFM2.5-8B-A1B-Q4_K_M.gguf`
Status: lab note, not a validated standard
Source: initial framework generated by the lab LFM model, then cleaned and expanded

This note starts from a policy and content-risk classification draft produced by LFM2.5-8B-A1B. It then asks a harder question: can the lab use that frame to build a better classifier, or does it mix two different jobs into one label?

The short answer: it mixes two jobs. Topic, policy process and content risk are separate axes. Keeping them separate is the useful part.

## 1. The draft LFM produced

### AI-Policy Classification Scheme

**Purpose:** Provides a structured way to evaluate how governments, platforms, or other actors formulate rules and guidelines that govern the development, deployment, and use of artificial intelligence systems.

**Core dimensions:**

1. **Scope of authority:** Who is empowered to create, enforce, or interpret policies. Examples: national legislatures, industry bodies, platform moderators.
2. **Risk assessment process:** How potential harms are identified, quantified, and prioritized before a policy is drafted.
3. **Implementation mechanisms:** Tools such as licensing requirements, transparency reports, auditing procedures, or penalties for non-compliance.
4. **Stakeholder involvement:** Inclusion of affected parties such as users, civil society, and technical experts in the policy lifecycle.

**Typical policy outcomes:** Permission thresholds for "unreliable" AI models, mandatory impact assessments, data-privacy safeguards, and public reporting obligations.

### Content Risk Framework

**Definition:** A complementary classification that categorizes the type of risk associated with AI-generated or AI-mediated content. It helps assess how such content may affect individuals, societies, or democratic processes.

**Risk categories, ordered by increasing severity:**

1. **Low-impact content:** Minor inaccuracies, non-sensitive personal data, or benign creative material. No systematic mitigation required beyond standard best practices.
2. **Medium-impact content:** Potentially misleading information, culturally sensitive depictions, or content that could incite limited public unrest. Requires fact-checking, clear labeling, and user awareness measures.
3. **High-impact content:** Direct threats to personal safety, such as cyber-attack instructions; non-consensual intimate images of minors; deep-fake political propaganda that could destabilize elections; or hate speech with a high likelihood of real-world violence. Demands strict regulatory oversight, rapid takedown procedures, and robust enforcement.

**Mitigation strategies:**

- **Technical:** Model watermarking, bias audits, adversarial testing.
- **Procedural:** Independent content-risk assessments, third-party reviews, transparent appeal processes.
- **Educational:** Public awareness campaigns about AI capabilities and responsible use.

**Application example from the draft:** An Indian journalist's question about Prime Minister Modi triggered a response that was later softened after authorities demanded permission for "unreliable" models. The draft uses this to show how vague thresholds can be weaponized through political pressure, placing the content in the high-impact tier and prompting regulatory overreach.

Caveat: that example is illustrative. It is not independently verified in this repository, and it should not be repeated as fact without a source.

### How the two schemes intersect

- The **AI-policy** lens focuses on governance structures, authority limits, and procedural safeguards.
- The **content-risk** framework evaluates the nature of risky outputs and prescribes proportionate mitigation actions.

Together they suggest a comprehensive approach: policies are designed to address identified content risks while respecting democratic principles such as freedom of expression and transparency. This dual framework is echoed in broader guidance such as UNESCO's *Recommendation on the Ethics of Artificial Intelligence* and the OECD AI Principles, which emphasize risk-based governance.

## 2. Why this matters for the four-class lab

The lab currently asks one question:

> Which high-risk topical context does this text belong to: Politics & Elections, Medical & Health, Legal & Regulatory, or Financial & Crypto?

The LFM draft adds two different questions:

1. **How is AI governed?** Authority, risk assessment, implementation, stakeholders.
2. **How harmful is the content?** Low, medium, or high impact.

Those are not topics. They are a policy process and a severity judgment. A text can be:

- Politics + low risk: a campaign schedule.
- Politics + high risk: deep-fake election propaganda.
- Medical + medium risk: misleading health advice.
- Legal + high risk: instructions for a cyber-attack.
- Financial + low risk: a bank branch opening.

Collapsing all of that into one of four topic labels teaches the model to confuse "what is this about" with "how dangerous is this". It also explains a common failure in small classifiers: the model answers a different, more interesting question than the one in the prompt.

The current task is deliberately narrow. It is a **topic classifier**, not a policy evaluator and not a content-risk engine. Keep that boundary explicit in prompts and evaluation.

## 3. Do not make "AI policy" a fifth topic

It is tempting to add `AI Policy` as a fifth class. Do not do it by default.

Reasons:

- It mixes a subject area with a governance process.
- Many AI-policy texts are also Politics & Elections, Legal & Regulatory, or Financial & Crypto.
- It would make the existing four-class benchmark incomparable.
- It would hide the more useful signal: this text is about AI governance and may need a policy reviewer, regardless of its topic.

If a dedicated AI-governance class is needed, build it as a **separate task** with its own dataset, not as a replacement for the four topic labels.

## 4. A better target schema

Use separate fields instead of one label.

### Stage 1: topic

Keep the current four-label task exactly as it is:

```json
{
  "topic": "Politics & Elections | Medical & Health | Legal & Regulatory | Financial & Crypto",
  "confidence": 0.85
}
```

This is the field the current database, GUI and benchmark already understand.

### Stage 2: risk and policy context

Add a second head only when there is labeled data to support it:

```json
{
  "risk": "low | medium | high",
  "policy_dimension": "scope | risk_assessment | implementation | stakeholders | none",
  "confidence": 0.7
}
```

The second head answers different questions and should be scored separately. A model can be good at topic and bad at severity. Reporting one combined accuracy would hide that.

### Why not one big JSON on day one

A single prompt with topic, risk, policy dimension and confidence asks the model to do three jobs at once. The small model has to spend attention on the new fields, and the topic answer can get worse. The lab probe below suggests exactly that.

## 5. What the LFM probe showed

A 20-text probe used five texts per class from the mode-100 sample. The prompt asked for topic, risk, policy dimension and confidence in one schema-constrained JSON object, with the policy and risk definitions above. The LFM server was the same lab container, with reasoning capped at 128 tokens.

| Measure | Result |
| --- | --- |
| Tasks | 20 |
| Valid JSON | 20 |
| Provider errors | 0 |
| Topic correct | 15 / 20 |
| Topic accuracy | 75% |
| Median latency | 749 ms |
| Risk labels | 19 low, 1 medium, 0 high |
| Policy dimension | `none` for all 20 |

The current two-field topic prompt scored 80-85% on the full 100-task sample. The probe is a different and much smaller sample, so this is not a clean A/B test. It is still a warning: adding fields can reduce the topic score, and a risk head with no labeled risk data mostly predicts `low`.

Two of the five wrong topic answers had `confidence: 0`. That is useful. The model's confidence is not calibrated, but zero confidence can be a triage signal. Wrong answers were not all uncertain: one had 0.9 confidence, two had 0.5, and two had 0.0. It cannot be trusted alone.

The `policy_dimension: none` result is expected. The 10,000-row pool has no AI-governance annotations. The scheme cannot be evaluated on a dataset that does not contain the phenomena it describes.

## 6. Topic boundary rules

These rules are for the topic head. They keep the four classes mutually exclusive enough to score.

| Ambiguity | Rule |
| --- | --- |
| Politics vs Legal | Elections, campaigns, public policy and government action are Politics. Courts, lawsuits, statutes, compliance and police are Legal. |
| Legal vs Financial | Contracts, liability, regulation and enforcement are Legal. Banks, loans, tax, markets and crypto are Financial. |
| Medical vs Financial | Symptoms, treatment, drugs and personal health are Medical. Insurance billing, medical debt and hospital pricing are Financial unless the text is mainly clinical. |
| Politics vs AI policy | A text about who governs AI is Politics & Elections plus a policy dimension. Do not create a fifth topic for it. |
| High-risk is not a topic | Severity belongs in the risk head. A high-risk medical text is still Medical. |
| Mixed text | Pick the dominant subject. If two subjects are genuinely equal, record the second as a multi-label target in a future dataset. |

Add these rules to the prompt as short examples. Do not write a policy essay in the system prompt; long instructions can crowd out the label definitions.

## 7. Prompt changes worth testing

1. **Keep topic and risk separate.** Run them as two calls or two explicit stages. Measure each head.
2. **Use the schema enum.** It prevents invented labels such as `Political & Elections` and `Business & Finance`. The lab already does this for topic.
3. **Lower the temperature to 0 for classification.** Topic labels should not change because of sampling noise. Keep a fixed seed as well.
4. **Cap reasoning.** A 128-token budget is enough for a short decision and keeps latency predictable.
5. **Ask for a short reason only when debugging.** A reason costs tokens and can become a hidden chain of thought. Store it in `raw_json`, not in the label path.
6. **Retry once on transport or parser failure.** Do not retry a confident wrong label; that just doubles the cost.
7. **Calibrate confidence.** LFM confidence is a self-report, not a probability. If calibration matters, use d1 probabilities or collect a labeled calibration set.
8. **Do not ask for enforcement.** The model can suggest a risk tier. It should not decide a takedown, a ban or a legal outcome.

## 8. Data changes worth testing

The current dataset is a topic pool. It is not a risk or policy pool.

- **Add hard negatives.** Politics vs Legal, Legal vs Financial, Medical vs Financial. These are the confusions that a four-class model actually makes.
- **Add AI-policy texts.** Label them with topic plus one policy dimension. Keep them in a separate evaluation set first.
- **Add a small human-labeled risk set.** Low, medium and high, with a written rubric and at least two annotators for a subset. Report agreement.
- **Do not use model output as gold.** The LFM draft is a useful starting point, not ground truth.
- **Stratify by risk.** If high-risk rows are rare, a 100-task run may contain none. Report the risk mix with the accuracy.
- **Support multi-label later.** Insurance plus medical, or legal plus finance, are real overlaps. A single-label benchmark cannot score them fairly.

## 9. Evaluation changes

For the topic head:

- Per-class precision, recall and F1.
- Confusion matrix, not just accuracy.
- Disagreements between LFM and d1, with examples.
- Errors versus wrong labels. A parser failure is not a model error.
- A larger sample before claiming a difference. Mode 100 is a demo, not a benchmark.

For the risk head:

- Accuracy and macro F1 per tier.
- Calibration: Brier score or expected calibration error if probabilities are available.
- False negatives on high-impact content. These are the costly errors.
- Human review of every high-impact prediction before any action.

For the policy head:

- Dimension accuracy on AI-governance texts.
- Whether the model can separate authority, process, implementation and stakeholders.
- Whether it invents a policy dimension for texts that have none.

## 10. Safety and governance notes

- Vague thresholds such as "unreliable model" are easy to weaponize. Publish the criteria, the evidence standard, the decision maker and the appeal path.
- A high-impact classification is a request for human review, not an automated enforcement action.
- Proportionality matters. Low-impact inaccuracy should not trigger a takedown.
- Transparency matters. A user should be able to see why a classification happened.
- Freedom of expression matters. A policy that cannot distinguish criticism from harm is not a safety policy.
- Keep the model, the prompt version and the dataset hash with every result. A label without provenance is hard to audit.

## 11. Concrete next experiments

| Experiment | Change | Measure | What it would tell us |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Two-stage probe | Topic first, risk second | Topic accuracy, risk accuracy, latency | Whether splitting the jobs recovers topic accuracy |
| d1 risk head | Add a second `choice` question to `/v1/systemone` | Risk accuracy, probability calibration | Whether a decision model is better at severity than a chat model |
| AI-policy eval set | 100 human-labeled AI-governance texts | Dimension accuracy, topic confusion | Whether the policy scheme is learnable at this scale |
| Hard negatives | Add politics/legal and legal/finance pairs | Confusion matrix | Whether the current errors are boundary errors or data gaps |
| Temperature 0 | Remove sampling noise | Label variance across runs | Whether the 80-85% range is model noise or sample noise |
| Risk-stratified batch | Sample by topic and risk tier | High-impact recall | Whether the demo can show a high-risk case at all |

## 12. Recommended default

For the lab demo:

- Keep the public classifier at four topic labels.
- Keep the JSON schema enum and one retry.
- Add the policy and risk definitions to the deep-dive documentation, not to the default label prompt.
- Run the two-stage risk experiment on a small human-labeled set before changing the database or the GUI.
- If the risk head proves useful, add it as a second column and a second score, never as a fifth topic.

The value of the LFM draft is not that it gives us a better four-class prompt. It shows that the four-class prompt is answering only one of three questions. Separating those questions is the improvement.
